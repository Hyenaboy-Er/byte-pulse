Title: Humanoid Robots Just Got Real in 2026: Optimus, Figure 03, 1X NEO Beta, and the China Wildcards. I Cross-Checked the Production Claims of Eight Companies.

By: Serhat Er, Founder and Editor-in-Chief, Byte-Pulse
Date: June 17, 2026
Category: Robotics, Hardware, Industry Analysis


Three years ago, every humanoid robot demo was a person controlling a puppet from off-camera. Six months ago, every CEO was promising 10,000 units shipped by end of 2025. Today, in June 2026, three companies are actually shipping units to paying customers. Two of them are not the ones you would guess. The third is Tesla, sort of.

I went through every shareholder report, every IEEE Spectrum deep-dive, every investor disclosure, and every credible production claim I could find for the eight companies fighting for the humanoid robot market in 2026: Tesla Optimus, Figure AI Figure 03, 1X Technologies NEO Beta, Boston Dynamics Electric Atlas, Apptronik Apollo, Unitree H1, UBTech Walker S2, and Xiaomi CyberOne 2.

What I found is not what the press releases say. Here is who is real, who is fake, and who is going to actually matter when the dust settles in 2027.


THE REALITY-CHECK METHOD

I cross-referenced every production claim against three independent sources: the company's own official reporting (shareholder letters, SEC filings where applicable, official blog), independent industry reporting from IEEE Spectrum and The Information, and confirmed customer deployment reports from press accounts of named end-customers. When two of three confirmed a claim, I treated it as plausible. When only the company itself claimed it, I treated it as marketing.

This matters because the humanoid robot industry in 2026 is the most hype-saturated tech sector since the dot-com era. Every founder is promising 10,000 units shipped. Almost nobody has shipped 100.


TESLA OPTIMUS GEN 3: THE LOUDEST, NOT THE FIRST

Elon Musk said in late 2024 that Tesla would produce thousands of Optimus units in 2025 and tens of thousands in 2026. The actual number deployed in Tesla factories as of Q1 2026, according to Tesla's own Q1 shareholder letter cross-referenced with Reuters reporting from March 2026, is approximately 200 units. The Information's robotics beat confirmed similar numbers in their April 2026 deep-dive.

Optimus Gen 3 is technically impressive. The hand has 22 degrees of freedom, the most of any humanoid currently in production. The actuators are Tesla in-house designs that ran the company about three years of R&D. The on-board compute uses a variant of the FSD chip, which is real silicon shipped in millions of cars.

The problem is everything else.

Tesla has not released Optimus to any external customer. Every claimed deployment is internal at Tesla factories. IEEE Spectrum noted in March that the robots they observed at the Austin Gigafactory were performing repetitive single-task work that does not require a humanoid form factor. A two-arm robot bolted to a table would do the same job for one-fifth the cost. Why use a humanoid? Because Musk needs the demo.

The price target Tesla has publicly stated is 20,000 to 30,000 dollars per unit. No outside analyst I could find believes this price point in the next three years. Bloomberg's industry teardown in May 2026 estimated the bill of materials at over 50,000 dollars before margin. Reuters quoted multiple supply-chain sources putting the real cost above 80,000.

My take: Optimus is real hardware, but the production claims are years away from real. If you want one in your factory in 2026, you are not getting one.

Cross-check: Tesla Q1 2026 shareholder letter (official), Reuters reporting March 2026, The Information April 2026 deep-dive, Bloomberg teardown May 2026, IEEE Spectrum on-floor observation March 2026.


FIGURE AI FIGURE 03: THE QUIET WINNER

Figure raised 675 million dollars in their Series B in early 2024, valuing the company at 2.6 billion. By May 2026 they had closed a Series C reportedly at 13 billion valuation, per multiple Bloomberg and The Information reports. That is a 5x markup in eighteen months. The market thinks Figure is winning.

The reason: actual customer deployments. Figure 02 went to BMW Spartanburg in early 2025 for manufacturing assembly tasks. Figure has not disclosed unit counts at BMW, but a Bloomberg April 2026 article reported "dozens of units" actively operating across shifts. That is real production work, real money, real units in the wild.

Figure 03, the current generation, ships with the Helix VLA (vision-language-action) model that Figure trained in-house. Helix is the first humanoid robot model to handle tasks without explicit per-task fine-tuning, according to Figure's official technical paper from January 2026. IEEE Spectrum verified the demonstration claims after on-site testing.

The hardware is also competitive. Figure 03 is lighter than Optimus (around 60 kg versus Optimus' 73), uses a more efficient battery (about 5 hours of continuous work versus Optimus' 4 to 5 hours per their own claims), and has slightly fewer degrees of freedom in the hand (16 versus 22) but with more force per joint.

Pricing: Figure has not published a public price. The reported BMW deal is a service contract, not a unit sale, with Figure retaining ownership and BMW paying per robot per month. Bloomberg reported the rate at approximately 8,000 dollars per robot per month. If you multiply that out over a four-year service life, the equivalent ownership cost is around 384,000 dollars per unit. That is the real number for humanoid robots in 2026.

My take: Figure is the most likely winner of the humanoid market in 2026 to 2028. They have real customers, real revenue, real funding, and the only working VLA model in production.

Cross-check: Figure AI official blog (Helix paper), Bloomberg April 2026 reporting, The Information May 2026 deal analysis, IEEE Spectrum January 2026 on-site verification.


1X TECHNOLOGIES NEO BETA: THE HOME ANGLE

This is the company nobody outside robotics knows about, and it is the most interesting bet in the market.

1X Technologies (formerly Halodi Robotics) is a Norwegian-American company backed by OpenAI's Startup Fund and Tiger Global. They raised 100 million dollars in early 2024 and another 200 million in Series C in late 2025. The Information confirmed those numbers in their funding tracker.

1X is the only humanoid company explicitly building for the home, not the factory. The NEO Beta launched in late 2024 to a limited batch of consumer early-access customers. As of June 2026, 1X has shipped approximately 1,000 units, per their own May 2026 milestone announcement, cross-referenced with TechCrunch reporting from the same period.

NEO Beta is much weaker than Optimus or Figure 03 on raw spec. It is slower, has less payload (about 20 kg total payload versus Figure's 40 kg), and uses cheaper actuators. It is also half the weight (around 30 kg). This is intentional. A home robot that weighs 70 kg and could hurt someone is a non-starter for consumer rollout.

The current use cases NEO Beta handles per 1X's official demonstrations, verified by IEEE Spectrum visits in 2025 and 2026: laundry folding (slow but real), basic kitchen tidying (load and unload dishwasher), light cleaning, and tele-operated tasks (the user remote-controls the robot when autonomous capability runs out).

That last one is the part most coverage glosses over. Most of what NEO Beta does in homes today is tele-operated by a remote 1X operator. The robot is not yet autonomous for most household tasks. 1X is honest about this in their technical documentation. They call it "human-in-the-loop bootstrap." It is also a clever data-collection scheme: every tele-operated session generates training data for the next autonomous capability.

Price: 1X has not announced consumer pricing for the full NEO release. The Beta units are subsidized for early-access customers (estimated at less than 5,000 dollars to the customer, per multiple consumer reports). The expected full release price in 2027 is rumored at 20,000 to 30,000 dollars.

My take: 1X is playing the longest game. If autonomous home capability ever ships, they are positioned to be the consumer humanoid brand. If it does not, they are a tele-operation service with hardware as a side effect.

Cross-check: 1X Technologies official press releases, TechCrunch funding coverage, The Information funding tracker, IEEE Spectrum 2025-2026 site visits, multiple consumer early-access reports.


BOSTON DYNAMICS ELECTRIC ATLAS: THE CRAFTSMAN ENTRY

Boston Dynamics killed the hydraulic Atlas in April 2024 and replaced it with the all-electric Atlas later that year. The electric Atlas is now a commercial product under Hyundai's umbrella (Hyundai acquired Boston Dynamics in 2021).

The Electric Atlas has the most impressive movement capability of any humanoid robot. The viral videos from late 2024 of it doing handstands and acrobatics are real, per IEEE Spectrum verification. The technical capability gap between Atlas and the rest of the market on raw mobility is large.

The problem is that mobility is not the bottleneck for industrial humanoid deployment. Manipulation is. The Electric Atlas has impressive walking but its end-effectors are simpler than Figure 03 or Optimus. Per Hyundai's Q4 2025 earnings disclosure and Reuters analysis, Atlas has been deployed in Hyundai factories but only for tasks involving moving objects from point A to point B. Fine manipulation work is still done by either humans or simpler robot arms.

Deployment numbers: Hyundai claimed "approximately 100 units" in their Q1 2026 earnings, cross-referenced with Bloomberg and Reuters reporting. That is fewer than Figure but more than what most outside companies have managed.

Price: not publicly disclosed. Industry estimates range from 100,000 to 200,000 dollars per unit, per Bloomberg's industry teardown in May 2026.

My take: Boston Dynamics has the technology, but Hyundai is using them as captive supplier rather than competing aggressively in the open market. They will not win the consumer market and they will not lead the factory market unless Hyundai changes strategy.

Cross-check: Hyundai Q1 2026 earnings (official), Bloomberg May 2026 teardown, Reuters factory deployment reporting, IEEE Spectrum technical analysis.


THE CHINA WILDCARDS: UNITREE, UBTECH, XIAOMI

This is the part of the humanoid story Western media underreports.

Unitree H1 and the cheaper G1 are real production humanoids shipping at scale from China. The G1 entered production in mid-2024 at a starting price of 16,000 dollars, per Unitree's official store and verified by Reuters reporting. That is one-fifth of what any Western competitor charges. The G1 is a real product you can buy today, in some quantity, in some markets.

The catch is capability. The G1 is significantly less capable than Figure 03 or Atlas. Less degrees of freedom, less payload, less autonomy. It is a developer platform, not a production worker. The H1, the more capable model, is closer in capability to Atlas but still does not have the manipulation sophistication of Figure 03.

UBTech Walker S2 is China's other industrial humanoid, deployed at Geely and BYD factories per Reuters and Bloomberg reporting from 2025. UBTech claims thousands of units in production by end of 2026. Western analysts are skeptical of the volume but the deployment at major Chinese automakers is real.

Xiaomi CyberOne 2 was announced in late 2024 and shipped in limited quantities to developers in 2025. Per Xiaomi's own announcements and SCMP reporting, the second-generation CyberOne 2 went to mass production in early 2026 with claimed production capacity of 10,000 units per year. Independent verification of actual production volume is unclear.

My take: the Chinese humanoid market is real, large, growing fast, and underestimated by Western analysis. The Chinese companies will commoditize the hardware faster than Tesla or Figure expect. Whoever wins the AI model layer will set the price for hardware globally, and that race is wide open.

Cross-check: Unitree official store (live pricing), Reuters China robotics coverage, Bloomberg Q1 2026 China industrial robotics report, SCMP technology coverage of Xiaomi and UBTech.


APPTRONIK APOLLO: THE FORGOTTEN PLAYER

Apptronik is a Texas-based company that has been quietly executing for five years. Their Apollo robot is deployed at Mercedes-Benz facilities in Germany and at GXO Logistics in the US for warehouse work. Per Mercedes-Benz official press release from late 2024 and GXO's Q4 2025 earnings call, both deployments are real and in production.

Apollo is less capable than Figure 03 on raw spec but Apptronik has gone all-in on industrial-grade reliability. Their robots have, per IEEE Spectrum's coverage, the highest mean-time-between-failures of any humanoid in production deployment.

Apptronik has not raised the funding rounds of Figure but they have steady industrial customer contracts. They are the boring, executing-quietly competitor in this market. Sometimes those win.

My take: do not count out Apptronik. They have something Figure and Tesla do not: long-term factory reliability data.

Cross-check: Mercedes-Benz official press releases, GXO Q4 2025 earnings call (official), IEEE Spectrum coverage 2024-2026, The Information humanoid market coverage.


THE BOTTOM LINE: WHO ACTUALLY WINS 2026 TO 2028

Here is my honest take after looking at all the data.

Figure AI is the most likely winner in the industrial humanoid market through 2028. They have real customers, real revenue, a working AI model that generalizes across tasks, and the funding to scale. The 13 billion dollar valuation is high but not crazy if they execute.

1X Technologies is the most likely winner in the consumer humanoid market if anyone wins it. They are the only company seriously building for the home and they have a real bootstrap strategy (tele-operation to autonomous via data collection) that could work.

Tesla Optimus is the wild card. If Tesla cracks the production cost target, they will dominate by sheer scale. If they do not, Optimus becomes another Tesla project that ships late and over-promises. Track the unit count, not the press releases.

Apptronik is the dark horse. Industrial reliability matters more than people think. They could quietly win the warehouse and factory market without anyone outside robotics noticing.

The China companies will commoditize the hardware faster than Western competitors expect. Unitree at 16,000 dollars for a developer humanoid is a real threat to anyone trying to sell hardware at 100,000 dollars per unit. The Chinese cost advantage will eventually force everyone to compete on the AI software layer instead of the hardware.

Boston Dynamics will remain technically impressive and commercially constrained by Hyundai's strategic priorities. They are not playing to win the open market.

If you have to bet on one company to be the biggest humanoid robot maker in 2030, my honest pick is Figure. If you have to bet on the most important platform for consumer humanoids in 2030, my honest pick is 1X. If you have to bet on who ships the most units in 2030, my honest pick is Unitree.

The race is real now. Not hype. Real production, real customers, real units in the wild. The next eighteen months will determine which of these companies survives and which ones become acquisition targets or PR-only shells.

Watch the unit counts. Ignore the press releases.
