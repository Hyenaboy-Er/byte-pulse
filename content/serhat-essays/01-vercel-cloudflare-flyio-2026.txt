Title: Vercel, Cloudflare Pages, Fly.io: What Builders Actually Pay in 2026

By: Serhat Er, Founder and Editor-in-Chief, Byte-Pulse
Date: June 16, 2026
Category: Cloud Infrastructure


The dirty secret about hosting platforms in 2026 is that almost every founder I talk to has switched providers at least once in the last twelve months. Vercel raised prices in late 2024 and again in 2025. Cloudflare quietly turned Workers into a real platform. Fly.io shipped scale-to-zero and made Postgres affordable. The marketing pages will not tell you any of this. The pricing pages tell you half the story.

I run apps on Vercel and Fly.io. I have looked at Cloudflare Pages for at least two projects this year. Here is what I learned. With real numbers, real tradeoffs, and the questions you should ask before you sign up for anything.


THE 2026 PRICING LANDSCAPE IN ONE PARAGRAPH

If you build a small Next.js app with maybe 50,000 monthly visitors and one database, you can host it for 0 dollars on Cloudflare Pages, around 20 dollars per month on Vercel Hobby plus a third-party Postgres, or around 5 to 15 dollars on Fly.io with their managed Postgres. If your app gets a viral hit and goes to 500,000 visitors in a week, Cloudflare will still cost you almost nothing, Vercel might send you a bill for 300 to 800 dollars, and Fly.io will scale you up but you have to think about regions and instance sizes. That is the 2026 reality in one paragraph. Now let me explain why.


VERCEL: GREAT DX, EXPENSIVE WHEN IT WORKS

Vercel is the obvious choice for Next.js because Next.js was built by Vercel. The deployment experience is unmatched. You push to GitHub, your site is live in 90 seconds, preview URLs for every PR, edge network globally, image optimization, ISR caching, analytics built in. This is real value. Anyone who tells you Vercel is overpriced has not tried to build this stack on raw AWS.

The problem starts when you get traffic.

Vercel Hobby is free, but the moment you have a side project that someone actually uses, you hit the bandwidth limit (100 GB per month) or the function invocation limit (100,000 per day on Hobby, 1 million on Pro). Pro is 20 dollars per seat per month. That sounds reasonable until you read the small print on overages.

Real example from a side project I watched. A friend built a Next.js SaaS, got onto Product Hunt, did 600,000 page views in 48 hours. His Vercel bill: 412 dollars. Most of it was function invocations and image optimization. Not bandwidth. The image optimizer in particular is brutal: every unique image variant counts, and Next.js generates a lot of variants.

What Vercel does well in 2026:
- Edge network is fast and reliable. Their P99 latency is competitive with Cloudflare.
- Their Cron jobs (added in 2024) are clean and easy.
- Vercel Postgres (now built on Neon) is a real product, not a toy.
- Their preview environments for every PR remain the gold standard.

What Vercel does poorly:
- Pricing for high-traffic apps is not competitive. You pay 5-10x what equivalent compute costs on hetzner or fly.io.
- Their KV (now Upstash-backed) is fine but you can do better with Cloudflare KV or Redis on Fly.
- Lock-in is real. Building heavily on their middleware, ISR specifics, and image optimizer makes migration painful.

Verdict: Use Vercel if you build with Next.js and you do not want to think about infra. Stop using Vercel the moment you make 50 dollars per month from your app, because at that point the infra cost is fighting your margins.


CLOUDFLARE PAGES: THE 2026 SLEEPER WINNER

Cloudflare Pages was the underdog two years ago. In 2026 it is, in my opinion, the best hosting platform for early-stage projects. Almost nobody talks about it. Everybody should.

The economics are absurd. The free tier gives you unlimited bandwidth, unlimited requests, and 500 builds per month. You read that right. Unlimited bandwidth. The next tier ("Workers Paid", 5 dollars per month) gets you 10 million Worker requests per day, 5 million KV reads, R2 storage at $0.015 per GB. For 5 dollars per month, you have a globally distributed app that can handle a Product Hunt launch without you flinching.

The catch, and this is real: developer experience is worse than Vercel.

Next.js on Cloudflare Pages works, but it works through their adapter (@cloudflare/next-on-pages). Some Next.js features do not translate cleanly to Workers. The bundle size limits (1 MB after compression for free tier, 10 MB for paid) will bite you if you add too many dependencies. ISR is more limited. The deployment is fast but the preview environments do not feel as polished as Vercel.

What Cloudflare does well:
- Cost. Nothing else comes close.
- D1 (their SQLite-backed database) is now production-ready. It is not for every use case but for many apps it is enough.
- R2 storage with zero egress fees changes the economics of any image-heavy app.
- Workers at the edge are genuinely fast. P50 latency under 10ms in most regions.

What Cloudflare does poorly:
- Documentation gaps. You will Google something and find a 2023 forum thread.
- Cold starts on infrequently-used routes are slower than Vercel.
- D1 has limits (currently around 10 GB per database, query timeouts) that hit you eventually.
- Their CLI (wrangler) has rough edges.

Verdict: If you are building anything from scratch in 2026 and you can tolerate the rougher edges, Cloudflare Pages is the rational choice. The unlimited bandwidth alone is worth changing your stack for.


FLY.IO: THE FULL-STACK ESCAPE HATCH

Fly.io is for when you outgrow the serverless model. Or when you never wanted it in the first place. They give you actual VMs (now called Machines) that you can scale to zero, deploy globally, attach volumes to, and run Postgres on without it being a separate company.

I run a couple of side projects on Fly.io. The mental model is closer to Heroku circa 2015, but with proper region control and modern tooling.

What Fly gets right:
- Scale-to-zero means you pay nothing when nobody is using your app. A machine that sleeps is 0 dollars. This is huge for low-traffic side projects.
- Their managed Postgres (in 2026, they finally have a real managed Postgres product after years of "Postgres on Fly is your responsibility") is clean and cheap. A small dev cluster runs you about 15 dollars per month.
- Region control. You can deploy your app in Frankfurt and Atlanta and Tokyo, three machines, with one fly.toml file.
- They have a Tigris-based S3-compatible object storage now. Cheap egress.

What Fly gets wrong:
- Outages. They had a rough patch in 2024. Better in 2025-2026 but the reputation persists.
- Their pricing calculator is confusing. Machine size, volume size, bandwidth, Postgres replicas, all stack up.
- DX for the first deploy is harder than Vercel or Cloudflare. The fly.toml is yet another config file to learn.

For my use case (running Next.js apps with Postgres and some background workers), Fly.io comes out cheaper than Vercel and about the same as Cloudflare Pages plus a managed Postgres provider. The flexibility is the real win: I can SSH into a machine, run a one-off migration, attach a debugger. With Vercel and Cloudflare, the platform owns the runtime.

Verdict: Use Fly.io if you need real backend control, if you have non-trivial background jobs, if you want one provider for app and Postgres, or if your stack is not Next.js. Avoid Fly.io if you want the platform to handle everything and you do not want to learn another deployment config.


SIDE-BY-SIDE: THREE REAL SCENARIOS

Let me give you three concrete scenarios with real numbers.

Scenario 1: A personal blog with 5,000 monthly visitors.
- Vercel: 0 dollars (Hobby tier covers it).
- Cloudflare Pages: 0 dollars (Free tier).
- Fly.io: 0 dollars if you use scale-to-zero (you pay only when someone visits).

All three are free. Pick whichever DX you prefer. The winner here is just whoever you find easier to set up.

Scenario 2: An indie SaaS with 50,000 monthly visitors, 5,000 daily API calls, one Postgres database, image uploads.
- Vercel Pro (20 dollars) plus Neon Postgres (29 dollars) plus their image optimizer eating into your bandwidth = approx 60 to 90 dollars per month.
- Cloudflare Pages Paid (5 dollars) plus D1 (free for this size) plus R2 storage (basically free) = approx 5 to 15 dollars per month.
- Fly.io with one app machine plus one Postgres = approx 15 to 30 dollars per month.

Cloudflare wins on cost by a factor of 5-10x. Vercel wins on developer experience. Fly.io is the middle ground.

Scenario 3: A viral content site with 1 million monthly visitors, lots of images, ISR-heavy.
- Vercel: brace yourself for 500 to 1500 dollars per month.
- Cloudflare Pages: probably under 30 dollars per month.
- Fly.io with proper caching: 50 to 150 dollars per month depending on how aggressive you scale.

This is where the choice becomes existential. If your business model is ad-supported content, Vercel will eat your margins. Cloudflare is the only platform that lets ad-supported content actually work in 2026.


THE HIDDEN COSTS NOBODY MENTIONS

There are real costs none of these pricing pages tell you about.

Vercel: every Next.js minor version upgrade is a coin flip. Sometimes ISR behavior changes, sometimes middleware behavior changes, sometimes image optimization changes. You spend a Saturday debugging.

Cloudflare: the edge-runtime constraints in Workers mean some npm packages just do not work. You will discover this when you try to add a feature six months in. Plan for it.

Fly.io: you are responsible for OS patches in your container, for monitoring, for backups. If you do not set up backups for your Postgres, you will lose data one day. That is not paranoia, that is statistics.

Switching costs are real on all three. If you build heavily on platform-specific features (Vercel middleware, Cloudflare KV, Fly volumes), you cannot move without a rewrite.


MY ACTUAL TAKE FOR 2026

If I had to start from scratch today, knowing what I know now:

For prototypes and side projects: Cloudflare Pages. The free tier is generous beyond reason, and even paid is cheaper than your coffee budget.

For Next.js apps that I intend to make money from: Fly.io. Better economics at scale, more control, one provider for everything.

For client work where the client will be paying the bill anyway: Vercel. The DX is unbeatable and your time is worth more than the platform markup.

For anything content-heavy or ad-supported: Cloudflare, no question. The math does not work on Vercel for ad-supported content.

I keep some apps on Vercel because the rewrite cost is not worth it. But if I started anything new today, none of it would land on Vercel by default.


THE BOTTOM LINE

There is no "best" hosting platform in 2026. There is the right platform for what you are building and how much traffic you expect.

What is true: Vercel is the most expensive, has the best DX, and is the right answer when somebody else pays. Cloudflare is the cheapest by 5-10x, has rough edges, and is the right answer when you pay your own bill. Fly.io is in the middle, has more flexibility, and is the right answer when you outgrow serverless.

What is also true: every one of these platforms will be different in twelve months. The pricing pages will change, the feature gaps will close, new competitors will emerge. The right move in infrastructure is to stay liquid: build with portability in mind, do not couple too tightly to platform features, keep your data layer independent.

Pick your platform for the next twelve months, not for the next ten years. Switch when the math stops working.
